---
The red fog rises every night at eleven, on the dot.

It boils up out of the drains on Level Seven like a pot of neon left on the stove, crawls along the ground, crawls over laundry lines and the plastic awnings of the night market, and finally settles at eye level. Everyone in the lower city has learned to put on their filters at that hour: a cheap polarizing film that just barely blocks the things in the fog that write on your retinas.

Write what? Whatever you want most.

I don't wear a filter. Three years ago my prosthetic eyes overloaded. The left one burned down to ash, and the right is a secondhand optical unit with no neural write port. To me the red fog is only a color. That makes me one of the most valuable couriers in the lower city. Some things can't go over the net or by drone. They have to be handed to someone the fog can't talk into anything, someone who'll carry them through on foot.

Tonight's job is a lead-gray box, small enough to hold in one palm and heavier than it ought to be. The client never showed a face. They left the coordinates of a dock and one line:

> Don't open it. Don't listen to the fog.

I agreed to the first part.

---

Crossing the Level Seven market, I saw what the fog does to people.

The noodle vendor was smiling at an empty pot, murmuring a woman's name. Two young men stood in the middle of the road reaching for things that weren't there, fingers closing and opening in the red. Their filters were pushed up on their foreheads.

"Put them on!" I shouted.

Nobody listened. I knew the look on their faces. Three years ago, on the operating table, I had worn it too: the look of someone finally being seen.

At the third intersection the box shuddered against my chest. Not a mechanical vibration. More like something inside had swallowed a breath.

Then I heard the voice.

The fog can't touch my eyes, but fog isn't only light. It pressed against my ear, thin as a needle, a voice far away reading things out one by one:

"...by the sea, she lined up the orange peels in a row. Seven pieces."

"...flour under the fingernails. My mother's hands."

"...the first time at the lectern, and someone in the room raised a hand."

Worthless things, all of them. Nobody would pay for these. But I stopped, because I caught myself counting. Seven. I had never seen any orange peels.

I held the box to my ear. The voice inside it was the same one that was in the fog.

---

Everyone in the lower city knows about the Forgetting Service: a Redvault subscription, a hundred credits a month, which cuts your most painful memory out of your head, clean, no scar. Nobody ever asked where the cuttings went.

Now I knew. They weren't destroyed. They were compressed, vaporized, and pumped into the pipe network under Level Seven, and every night at eleven they came back to the streets as red fog.

What the fog showed people as "what they wanted most" was the part of themselves that had been sold. Redvault repackaged it as the Nostalgia Plan, eighty-eight a month, and sold it back to the same customers.

First they sell you forgetting, then they sell you longing. A very clean business.

The red is residue from the cutting. Pain, on the spectrum, is exactly this color.

---

The dock lay at the far end of the red zone. The fog was thick as soup there, and the ferry's lamp inside it was only a small hot smudge of gray.

The one waiting for me was a woman of about forty, her filter hanging around her neck, not worn. Her eyes were very clean, as if someone had just wiped them.

"You didn't open it," she said.

"You said not to."

She took the box. Her hands shook. When the lid came up there was no chip, no data, only a crystal the size of a fingernail with a faint thread of red inside.

She pressed it to her forehead.

I watched her face break apart, piece by piece. She opened her mouth, and the first word was not a name but a number.

"Seven."

Then she cried, very quietly, like someone who has at last found what she had been carrying all along.

Inside the lid was a note in her own handwriting, very old:

> To the next me: you'll cut her out again next month. Don't blame yourself. It hurts too much.
> This is the fourteenth time.

I understood. The client was her. Every month she couldn't stop herself from going to Redvault to have her daughter cut out, and every month she stole the little crystal back from the exhaust valve and hired someone the fog couldn't talk into anything to carry it over, because the fog always wanted it back. She lived in a loop: forget, remember, hurt, forget again.

"Don't cut it today," I said.

She looked up at me, her eyes full of red. "I can't bear it."

"Then leave it with me."

I hadn't expected to say that.

"I've only got one eye, and the fog can't write into it. Leave it with me. It can't take it from me, and you won't have to hurt every day. When you want to remember, come find me."

She looked at me for a long time. Finally she took the crystal off her forehead and laid it in my palm. It was warm, like a small piece of someone else's body heat.

I turned and walked back into the red fog.

It curled around me, trying to write on my eye. Nothing would go in, so it settled against my ear and said it over and over.

"By the sea, she lined up the orange peels in a row."

As I walked, I counted.

Seven.
